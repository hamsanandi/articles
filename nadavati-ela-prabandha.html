Reconstructing the Nādāvatī Elā of Mānasollāsa:
A Study of Early Compositional Thought in South Indian Art Music
Ramaprasad K V
Abstract 1. Introduction 2. Mānasollāsa 3. Elā Prabandha 4. Previous Scholarship 5. Description 6. Analysis 7. Reconstruction 8. Conclusion
Abstract

The extant compositional repertoire of Karnāṭaka Saṅgīta is dominated by forms that have become popular in the last few centuries, while many compositions from earlier times are known primarily through textual and musicological sources. This paper undertakes a theoretical and musical reconstruction of one type of Elā prabandha, a historical compositional form described in Mānasollāsa, a twelfth-century Sanskrit treatise (also known as Abhilāṣitārtha Cintāmaṇi) written by the Cālukya ruler Someśvara.

1. Introduction

The textual tradition of Indian musicological writing begins with the Nātyaśastra. Within this historical continuum, Mānasollāsa composed in the late twelfth century CE occupies a particularly significant position. It offers a valuable window into earlier conceptions of musical form and classification.

The present study focuses on one of the compositional types, the Nādāvatī Elā described in the Mānasollāsa, examining its formal organization and proposing a reconstruction in a form that could be performed today.

2. Mānasollāsa and Early Compositional Classification

The Gīta-vinoda section of the Mānasollāsa provides a detailed account of the music practices of Someśvara's times. Here the author includes discussions of rāga, prosody (chandas), and a wide range of compositional forms.

Someśvara describes poetic meters used in several literary languages and outlines multiple categories of musical compositions collectively referred to as prabandhas. These prabandhas are metrically and structurally organized into different types. A notable feature of Mānasollāsa is the inclusion of example lyrics for many of the prabandha he describes. The diversity of compositions attests both to the richness of compositional forms and to the importance accorded to systematic classification.

3. The Elā Prabandha

Among the many classes of prabandhas described in Mānasollāsa, the Elā prabandha is of particular interest. Some of the Elā prabandhas, as defined in this text, exhibit structural and aesthetic features that suggest a closer relationship to later compositional forms in Karnāṭaka Saṅgīta such as the pada, dāru, tillānā, and kṛti.

Mānasollāsa classifies Elā prabandhas on structural grounds into three categories: Gaṇaila, Mātraila, and Varṇaila. In the Gaṇaila type of Elā prabandhas, the text is constructed according to specific gaṇa¹ patterns within the metrical scheme. Four types of Gaṇaila are enumerated: Nādāvatī, Nandāvatī, Bhadrāvatī, and Haṃsāvatī.

The present study is concerned exclusively with the Nādāvatī Elā.

4. Previous Scholarship and Scope of the Present Study

This work builds upon the study by Aniruddha et al.,* which presents a detailed textual and structural analysis of the Nādāvatī and Nandāvatī Elā prabandhas as described in Mānasollāsa. While that study does not attempt a musical reconstruction, it provides an essential foundation for understanding the formal characteristics of these compositions.

The present paper extends this line of inquiry by proposing a historically informed musical reconstruction of a Nādāvatī Elā. Given the absence of notated music (svaralipi) in Mānasollāsa, this reconstruction necessarily remains hypothetical; nevertheless, it is grounded in textual prescriptions concerning metrical structure and organization.

5. Description of the Nādāvatī Elā prabandha as given in the Mānasollāsa

In Chapter 16 of the fourth vimśati (Gīta-vinoda), Someśvara presents a detailed account of Elā prabandhas in verses 440–524 of the Mānasollāsa. Verses 440–447 establish a general structural template applicable to Elā prabandhas. Verses 448–451 specify the formal constraints of the Nādāvatī Elā (a subtype within the Gaṇaila class).

5.1 General Structure of the Elā prabandha

Someśvara defines the formal components of an Elā prabandha as comprising four principal sections: udgrāha, melāpaka, dhruva, and ābhoga (v. 440). The udgrāha, so called because the song "takes off" from it, serves as the opening section, while the melāpaka functions as a connective passage joining the udgrāha and the dhruva (v. 441). The dhruva is characterized as a recurring and invariant section within the composition, and its concluding portion is designated as the ābhoga (v. 442).

These terms are stated to apply to all gītas (songs), although the inclusion of the melāpaka and ābhoga is described as optional (v. 443). Structurally, the udgrāha comprises two padas² employing alliteration (anuprāsa), followed by a gamaka, and then another pada describing the subject matter of the composition. The melāpaka, when present, is to be rendered with ornamental gamakas (vv. 444–445). The dhruva that follows is explicitly stated to be free of gaṇa restrictions (vv. 446–447). The ābhoga, which typically contains the composer's signature, is sung thereafter, and the composition concludes with a repetition of the dhruva.

5.2 Structural Features of the Nādāvatī Elā

Following this generic exposition, verses 448–451 describe the structural features specific to the Nādāvatī Elā prabandha. Verse 448 explains the method by which the eight possible gaṇas (ya, ma, ta, ra, ja, bha, na, sa) are encoded through particular lexical indicators.

In the Nādāvatī Elā, a pada is constructed using five bha-gaṇas and one na-gaṇa (v. 449), accompanied by another pada employing anuprāsa. The composition consists of three such pādas in total³ (v. 450). Verses 452–453 prescribe that a properly constructed Nādāvatī Elā should be set to an appropriate rāga and rendered in manda tāla, with the intended aesthetic effect being the evocation of śṛṅgāra rasa in kaiśikī vṛtti, i.e., a graceful manner of delivery.

6. Analysis of the Example in the Mānasollāsa

6.1 Formal Segmentation and Compositional Function

The example provided in the Mānasollāsa is explicitly labeled according to the four-part template: udgrāha, melāpaka, dhruva, and ābhoga, indicating the practical usage of the terminology rather than merely abstract taxonomy. In particular, the dhruva is singled out as the element that "repeats itself many times" and remains invariant; the explicit marking of dhruva therefore provides a clear indication that it was repeated after each pāda (stanza). The inclusion of melāpaka and ābhoga, which are optional components for gītas in general, probably indicates that Someśvara wanted to illustrate a "fully articulated" Nādāvatī Elā prabandha.

6.2 Constraint and Freedom: the Distribution of gaṇa Regulation

The defining feature of Gaṇaila Elā prabandhas as given in the Mānasollāsa is the construction of padas under specified gaṇa patterns. The Nādāvatī subtype is specified as employing five bha-gaṇas and one na-gaṇa in the construction of a pada (v. 449). The dhruva is explicitly exempted from gaṇa restrictions.

The first two lines of each pāda of the composition follow the gaṇa restrictions, as shown below:

6.3 The Role of anuprāsa and gamaka

The text specifies that the udgrāha contains two padas "with alliteration," followed by a gamaka, and then another pada describing the object of the composition; when present, the melāpaka is likewise to be ornamented with gamakas. While some anuprāsa is seen in the example, it does not exhibit extensive alliteration. Since no notations are given, we cannot determine the exact gamaka or prayoga that was employed. A plausible reading is to imagine either precomposed svara patterns of equal length to other lines of the composition, or the performer would apply their manodharma to improvise with gamakas to fill the said space. This parallels similar practice in certain composition types in Hindustani music today.

6.4 Performance Prescription: rāga, tāla, and Aesthetic Aim

The Mānasollāsa prescribes that the Nādāvatī Elā should be set to an appropriate rāga and rendered in manda tāla, with the aim of evoking śṛṅgāra rasa in kaiśikī vṛtti (a graceful style). A discussion of manda tāla is outside the scope of this paper. Some manuscripts specify the rāga as ṭakka. Overall, the goal is to use a rāga and tāla appropriate to the subject of the composition — in this case, to create a romantic mode and a graceful delivery.

6.5 Interim Synthesis

The foregoing analysis yields a compact working model: the Nādāvatī Elā is described as a multi-pāda form with specified gaṇa constraints and alliterative design in the udgrāha, articulated by gamaka-marked transitions, expanded through optional connective and closing components (melāpaka, ābhoga), and anchored by a recurrent, invariant dhruva that is exempt from gaṇa restrictions. This provides the foundation for the reconstruction proposed in the subsequent section.

7. Musical Reconstruction: Composing a New Nādāvatī Elā prabandha

7.1 Aim and Status of the Reconstruction

The reconstruction proceeds on two complementary levels. First, a newly composed Kannada sāhitya is presented to make the formal grammar of the Nādāvatī Elā explicit. Second, a notated melodic outline is supplied so that the piece may be sung within contemporary Karnāṭaka Saṅgīta practice, while preserving the sectional structure and aesthetic orientation prescribed by the treatise. Taken together, these layers are offered as a proof of concept rather than an attempt at strict historical stylistic reconstruction.

7.2 Textual Realization: sāhitya

The newly composed Kannada sāhitya is a controlled textual instantiation designed to make the structure of the prabandha legible. Structurally, the setting follows a multi-pāda design articulated by a recurrent dhruva and a concluding ābhoga. The dhruva is written out at each point of recurrence. The Kannada text is presented alongside Roman transliteration and a brief English prose summary.

In accordance with the Mānasollāsa prescription, gaṇa regulations are not followed in the recurrent dhruva and concluding ābhoga portions.

7.3 Musical Realization of Nādāvatī Elā

The present reconstruction supplies a notated melodic outline for the sāhitya presented in Section 7.2, aiming to test its musical realizability within contemporary Karnāṭaka Saṅgīta practice. The notation is intentionally minimal, functioning as a baseline for singability and svara–sāhitya alignment rather than as a fully ornamented prescription. A performer may add judicious sangatis.

The reconstruction sets the composition in Mohana rāga and Rūpaka tāla. While the textual prescription specifies performance in manda tāla, the present setting adopts the alternate reading that allows an "appropriate rāga" and prioritizes rāga–text congruence in service of the treatise's stated aesthetic aim. Mohana suits the mood of the sāhitya presented here.

8. Conclusion

This study has presented a reconstruction of a Nādāvatī Elā, demonstrating that the compositional grammar described in the Mānasollāsa can be operationalized as a performable musical object under explicitly stated assumptions. Because several Haridāsa compositions exhibit repeated metrical designs and extensive alliteration, further comparative work may help determine whether any of the Elā prabandhas described in the Mānasollāsa and related musicological texts informed (directly or indirectly) the compositional practice of Haridāsa authors.

Finally, the recurrent dhruva of the Nādāvatī Elā invites a functional comparison with the pallavi of present-day kṛtis, while the closing ābhoga may be compared — cautiously — to the anupallavi. Such comparisons are heuristic rather than genealogical: they help articulate structural analogies between premodern prabandha templates and later compositional practices.
